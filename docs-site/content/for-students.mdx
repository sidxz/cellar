# For students & newcomers

**Who this is for:** Students, interns, and scientists new to drug discovery informatics who want to understand what chemcellar does and how the underlying concepts work.

**What you'll find here:** Plain-language explanations of the key ideas, with interactive examples where they help most.

---

## Start with the big picture

Drug discovery is the process of finding molecules that could become medicines. A typical discovery program involves thousands of compounds, hundreds of experiments, and many scientists working in parallel over years. Without a shared system, data lives in spreadsheets, notebooks, and individual hard drives — impossible to search, easy to lose, and difficult to audit.

chemcellar is that shared system. It tracks every compound from first synthesis to last decision, keeps a permanent record of every experiment, and lets the whole team search the data in seconds.

---

## Key concept 1: What is a molecule in chemcellar?

In everyday chemistry, "molecule" can mean many things. In chemcellar it means one specific thing: a unique chemical structure that has been formally registered in the system.

When a chemist synthesizes a new compound, they draw or paste its structure in [SMILES](/reference/glossary#smiles) notation — a text code that describes a 2D chemical structure. chemcellar converts it to a standard form and assigns a unique **[registration number](/reference/glossary#registration-number)** (e.g., `CV-00042`). That number never changes, and it is how everyone on the team refers to that compound.

## Key concept 2: Molecular properties

Every molecule has computable properties that predict how it will behave in the body. These are calculated from the structure alone — no wet chemistry needed. The most commonly used properties in early drug discovery are molecular weight (MW), lipophilicity (logP), polar surface area (TPSA), and hydrogen bond donor and acceptor counts (HBD/HBA).

These five numbers form **Lipinski's Rule of Five** — a rough filter for whether a compound might survive as an oral drug. MW under 500, logP between 1 and 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10, and TPSA under 120 Å² are the thresholds. It is a heuristic, not a law; many successful drugs violate one or more rules.

You can inspect computed properties on any molecule's detail page inside chemcellar. The [Concepts → Molecular properties](/concepts/molecular-properties) page explains each descriptor in detail.

## Key concept 3: Batches and samples

The same molecule can be synthesized many times by different chemists, on different days, at different purities. Each distinct synthesis run is called a **[batch](/reference/glossary#batch)**.

Think of it this way:

- **Molecule** = the recipe (the structure, the identity)
- **Batch** = a jar of the compound made on a particular day (specific purity, weight, date)
- **Sample** = an individual aliquot drawn from that jar and placed in a specific vial in the freezer

chemcellar tracks all three. When you run an assay, you record which batch (and often which sample) you used. That lets you trace any result back to the exact material tested.

## Key concept 4: Assays, plates, and wells

An **assay** is an experiment that measures how a compound affects a biological system — usually a protein or a cell line. In a typical high-throughput screen, many compounds are tested simultaneously in a **[microplate](/reference/glossary#plate)** — a plastic tray with 96 or 384 small wells, each containing a tiny volume of compound and biological reagent.

After the experiment, a plate reader measures each well (fluorescence, luminescence, or absorbance) and produces a file of numbers — one per well. chemcellar stores those numbers, associates each well with the compound and concentration tested, and displays the results as a color-coded heatmap. Darker colors typically indicate higher activity in an inhibition assay.

## Key concept 5: Dose-response curves and IC50

When scientists want to know how potent a compound is, they test it at several different concentrations and measure the effect at each. As concentration increases, effect typically increases — until the system is fully inhibited. Plotting effect (% inhibition) against concentration on a log scale gives an S-shaped curve called a **[dose-response curve](/reference/glossary#dose-response-curve)**.

The most important number from this curve is the **[IC50](/reference/glossary#ic50)** — the concentration at which 50% of the maximum effect is achieved. A lower IC50 means a more potent compound.

chemcellar fits these curves automatically using a **four-parameter logistic (4PL)** model:

- **Bottom** — the effect at very low concentration (ideally 0% inhibition)
- **Top** — the effect at very high concentration (ideally 100% inhibition)
- **IC50** — the midpoint concentration
- **Hill slope** — how steeply the curve transitions (a slope of 1 gives a standard sigmoidal shape)

## Key concept 6: Structure similarity

Not all interesting compounds are identical — often you want to find compounds that are *similar* to a known active. chemcellar computes structural similarity using **[Tanimoto](/reference/glossary#tanimoto) similarity** over Morgan fingerprints.

A fingerprint is a binary string that encodes the structural features present in a molecule. The Tanimoto coefficient counts how many features two molecules share, divided by the total features in either. A value of 1.0 means identical; 0.0 means nothing in common. In practice, compounds with Tanimoto above 0.7 are considered structurally similar and often have related biological activity.

You can run a similarity search from the main compound search in chemcellar — draw or paste a reference structure, set a threshold, and see all matching compounds with their scores.

## Key concept 7: The audit trail and data integrity

Every action in chemcellar is recorded in an **append-only audit trail**. This means you can always see who entered which data, when, and what they changed. Records cannot be quietly deleted or overwritten — corrections create new entries that reference the original.

This matters for drug discovery because:

- **Reproducibility** — you can trace any result to the exact experiment, material, and scientist.
- **Regulatory compliance** — pharmaceutical development is governed by regulations (21 CFR Part 11 in the US) that require electronic records to be trustworthy and attributable.
- **Trust** — when a program team decides to advance a compound, they need to be confident the data is real and correct.

<Callout type="warning">If you think data in an approved run or closed campaign is wrong, do not try to edit it directly. Report it to your supervisor — corrections require administrator authorization and are recorded in the audit trail.</Callout>
