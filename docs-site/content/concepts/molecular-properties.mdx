# Molecular properties

**What you'll learn:** The key physicochemical descriptors chemcellar computes for every molecule, the difference between computed descriptors and predicted properties, how Lipinski's Rule of Five relates to drug-likeness, and how to use the live property calculator.

---

## Why molecular properties matter

A compound's physical and chemical properties determine how it moves through biological systems — how well it is absorbed, how it distributes across tissues, whether it can cross a membrane, and how quickly the body eliminates it. Even before any biology is done, a handful of calculated numbers can flag whether a compound is likely to behave as an oral drug or whether it will have practical problems in an assay.

chemcellar computes a standard set of properties for every [molecule](/reference/glossary#molecule) the moment it is registered. You can see these numbers on the molecule detail page, filter the compound list by them, and export them alongside screening data.

---

## Computed descriptors vs predicted properties

chemcellar distinguishes two categories of molecular properties.

### Computed descriptors

**Computed descriptors** are derived deterministically from the molecular graph using RDKit. Given the same structure, the same algorithm always produces the same number — no external service, no model, no uncertainty. chemcellar computes these synchronously during registration, in roughly ten milliseconds per molecule.

The full set of computed descriptors is described in the table below.

### Predicted properties

**Predicted properties** such as logD, pKa, and aqueous solubility (logS) require either a pH context, a specialised prediction engine, or a machine-learning model. They are not trivially derivable from the 2D structure alone. chemcellar stores these as optional fields (`logd`, `pka`, `logs`) that may be populated later — for example, from a connected prediction service or a manually uploaded result. They are nullable independently: a molecule may have logD but not pKa, or neither.

When predicted properties are present, chemcellar also records which tool or model produced them and when, so you always know the provenance.

<Callout type="info">
The property values shown on the molecule detail page are **computed descriptors** unless explicitly labelled as predicted. Computed descriptors are always available for disclosed molecules. Predicted properties may be blank if no prediction service is configured.
</Callout>

## The core computed descriptors

### Molecular weight (MW)

Molecular weight is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in the molecule, expressed in daltons (Da). It is one of the most fundamental properties: large molecules typically have poor oral absorption.

Lipinski's guideline (see [Rule of Five](#rule-of-five) below) sets the upper limit at **500 Da** for orally absorbed drugs.

chemcellar uses the average molecular weight (not the monoisotopic mass). The exact monoisotopic mass is also stored and available in exports.

### logP (lipophilicity)

logP is the logarithm of the partition coefficient between octanol and water — a measure of how hydrophobic (fat-loving) a molecule is. A high logP means the molecule prefers organic solvents over water; a low or negative logP means it prefers water.

| logP range | Rough interpretation |
|-----------|---------------------|
| < 0 | Highly hydrophilic; may have poor membrane permeability |
| 0 – 3 | Good balance; often associated with oral absorption |
| 3 – 5 | Increasingly lipophilic; watch for solubility issues |
| > 5 | Highly lipophilic; high risk of poor aqueous solubility and promiscuous binding |

chemcellar computes logP using RDKit's Crippen method, which is a deterministic fragment-based calculation. This is **not** a predicted logD (which would require knowing the ionisation state at a specific pH). The Crippen logP assumes the neutral form of the molecule.

### TPSA (topological polar surface area)

[TPSA](/reference/glossary#tpsa) is the surface area of a molecule contributed by polar atoms — primarily oxygen, nitrogen, and their attached hydrogens. It is a 2D calculation (not a 3D surface) and is a strong predictor of membrane permeability and CNS penetration.

- **TPSA < 60 Å²** — generally associated with CNS penetration.
- **TPSA < 140 Å²** — associated with good oral absorption.
- **TPSA > 140 Å²** — often correlates with poor absorption.

### HBD (hydrogen bond donors)

[HBD](/reference/glossary#hbd) counts the number of nitrogen and oxygen atoms that carry at least one hydrogen — these atoms can donate a hydrogen bond to a receptor. Each HBD costs energy to desolvate when crossing a lipophilic membrane.

Lipinski's guideline: **≤ 5 HBD** for orally absorbed drugs.

### HBA (hydrogen bond acceptors)

[HBA](/reference/glossary#hba) counts the number of nitrogen and oxygen atoms in the molecule — these can accept a hydrogen bond. A high HBA count often correlates with high TPSA and poor membrane permeability.

Lipinski's guideline: **≤ 10 HBA**.

### Other descriptors

chemcellar also computes and stores:

| Descriptor | What it counts |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Rotatable bonds** | Bonds that allow free rotation; more rotatable bonds → more conformational flexibility → often worse oral bioavailability (Veber's rule: ≤ 10) |
| **Aromatic rings** | Number of aromatic ring systems |
| **Ring count** | Total number of rings of any kind |
| **Heavy atom count** | Non-hydrogen atoms (a simple proxy for molecular size) |
| **Molecular formula** | e.g., C₉H₈O₄ for aspirin |
| **Exact mass** | Monoisotopic mass (used in mass spectrometry identification) |

## Rule of Five

Lipinski's Rule of Five (Ro5) is an empirical rule of thumb proposed by Christopher Lipinski at Pfizer in 1997. It states that an orally absorbed drug is likely to have:

- Molecular weight ≤ 500 Da
- logP ≤ 5
- HBD ≤ 5
- HBA ≤ 10

Each limit is a multiple of five — hence the name. A molecule that violates more than one of these rules is predicted to have poor oral bioavailability. The rule was derived from an analysis of approved drugs and is a widely used early filter in medicinal chemistry.

chemcellar computes an **Ro5 violation count** (0–4) for every molecule. You can filter the compound list by this count to identify problematic compounds early.

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
The Rule of Five is a guideline, not a hard cutoff. Many excellent drugs violate one or more rules — for example, macrolide antibiotics and natural products. Use it as a flag for further investigation, not as a pass/fail decision.
</Callout>

## Where properties appear in the UI

### Molecule detail page

The **Properties** panel on the molecule detail page shows all computed descriptors in a structured card. Predicted properties (when available) appear in a separate **Predicted** card with the source and date labelled.

> 📷 *Screenshot: the molecule detail page Properties panel, showing MW, logP, TPSA, HBD, HBA, and Ro5 violation count for a representative compound.*

### Molecule list (compound registry)

You can add property columns — MW, logP, TPSA, HBD, HBA — to the compound list grid using the **Columns** menu. Once visible, the columns are sortable and filterable. This is useful for quick lead series triage.

> 📷 *Screenshot: the compound registry AG Grid with MW and logP columns enabled, showing a filtered view of compounds with logP between 2 and 4.*

### Search & filter

The [search](/guides/search) interface lets you restrict results to a numerical range for any computed descriptor — for example, molecules with MW between 300 and 450 Da and TPSA below 90 Å². Combine property filters with structure filters (substructure or similarity) for multi-parameter screening.

## Properties for undisclosed molecules

[Undisclosed molecules](/concepts/undisclosed-molecules) — compounds registered without a revealed structure — have no computed descriptors or predicted properties. All property fields are blank until the structure is disclosed. After disclosure (and any resulting merge), descriptors are computed immediately.

<Callout type="info">
An undisclosed molecule can still participate in screening assays and appear in runs. It simply will not appear in property-based filter results until its structure is revealed.
</Callout>

## Accuracy and limitations

- **Computed descriptors** are exact given the structure as drawn. Accuracy depends on the quality of the input — a wrong stereo assignment or an incorrect salt strip will produce a subtly wrong MW or logP.
- **Crippen logP** is a fragment-based 2D calculation. It is fast and reproducible but may diverge from experimentally measured logP, especially for unusual functional groups or highly polar compounds.
- **TPSA** as implemented is a topological (2D) approximation. It correlates well with 3D-computed PSA for drug-like molecules but is less accurate for macrocycles or unusual scaffolds.
- **Predicted properties** carry the uncertainty of the underlying model. chemcellar records the source and date so you can judge how current and reliable the predictions are.
