# Concepts: cheminformatics & the compound registry

**What you'll learn:** The ideas that underpin chemcellar — what a compound registry does, why chemical identity is harder than it looks, and how the platform organises everything from a drawn structure to a screening result.

---

## What is a compound registry?

A compound registry is the master record of every chemical structure your organisation works with. Think of it like a library catalogue, but for molecules. Each unique structure gets one permanent record — its [molecule](/reference/glossary#molecule) — along with a human-readable registration number (for example, CV-00001). Every batch synthesised, every sample stored, and every assay result ever measured gets linked back to that record.

Without a registry, the same compound can appear under dozens of vendor codes, internal codes, and hand-typed names across spreadsheets and notebooks. A registry eliminates that ambiguity by anchoring every piece of scientific data to a single, deduplicated structural identity.

chemcellar is the registry for your workspace. It also manages the downstream data — [batches](/concepts/batches-and-samples), [inventory](/guides/inventory), [screening runs](/guides/screening), and [research organisation](/guides/research-organization/projects) — all connected to the same canonical compound record.

---

## Why chemical identity is non-trivial

A molecule drawn by one chemist may look different from the same compound drawn by another, even if the two structures are chemically identical. Several sources of ambiguity arise in practice:

- **Representation format.** A structure can be expressed as a hand-drawn image, a SMILES string, an InChI, a molfile, or a name. Different formats carry different amounts of information.
- **Canonicalization.** The same molecule can be written as many different SMILES strings. A canonical SMILES is the unique, agreed-upon form for a given structure — without it, a simple text comparison would falsely report duplicates as unique compounds.
- **Stereochemistry.** Two molecules with the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements of atoms (enantiomers, diastereomers) are distinct compounds with different biological activities. The registry must track stereo precisely.
- **Tautomers.** Many structures exist as interconverting forms in solution (for example, a keto form and an enol form). chemcellar uses standard InChI's mobile-hydrogen layer to treat these as the same compound without running an explicit tautomer canonicalizer — matching the practice of ChEMBL, PubChem, and CAS.
- **Salts.** A drug might be stored as its hydrochloride salt, but the parent free base is the molecule of interest. chemcellar strips counter-ions before deduplication, recording the salt form on the [batch](/reference/glossary#batch) rather than the molecule.

Understanding these distinctions helps you predict how chemcellar will handle your data at registration time.

## How chemcellar connects the dots

Every scientific action in chemcellar traces back to a molecule record:

```
Molecule (canonical structure, registration number)
  └── Batch (specific synthesis or purchase lot)
        └── Sample (physical aliquot in inventory)
              └── Well (position on a plate in a run)
                    └── ReadoutData (the measured value)
                          └── DoseResponseCurve (IC50, EC50)
```

A [project](/reference/glossary#project) or [collection](/reference/glossary#collection) groups molecules for scientific purposes. A [screen campaign](/reference/glossary#campaign) tracks which molecules were screened under a given [protocol](/reference/glossary#protocol) and what the outcomes were.

<Callout type="info">
chemcellar enforces workspace isolation — all records belong to one workspace and are never mixed with data from another. Your compounds are yours.
</Callout>
