# Dose-response & potency

**What you'll learn:** What a dose-response curve is, how chemcellar fits it, and what each
parameter — IC50, EC50, Hill slope, top, bottom — tells you about a compound's potency and behavior.

---

## What is a dose-response experiment?

In a **[dose-response experiment](/reference/glossary#dose-response)**, you test the same compound at several concentrations
and measure a biological effect at each one. The goal is to find how much of the compound is needed
to produce a defined level of response — typically 50% of the maximum possible effect.

A typical experiment spans six to ten concentrations, usually in a geometric series (e.g., 0.001,
0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30 µM). At low concentrations the compound has little
effect; at high concentrations the effect plateaus. The curve that connects these points carries
far more information than a single-concentration screen.

> ten wells across two rows, each at a successive dilution step.*

## The four-parameter logistic (4PL) model

chemcellar fits dose-response data using the **four-parameter logistic (4PL)** equation — the
industry-standard model for sigmoidal concentration-response relationships:

```
Response = Bottom + (Top - Bottom) / (1 + (IC50 / Concentration)^HillSlope)
```

The four parameters describe the full shape of the curve:

| Parameter | What it means |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Bottom** | The lower asymptote — the response at very high compound concentration (typically 0% in an inhibition assay) |
| **Top** | The upper asymptote — the response at zero compound concentration (typically 100%) |
| **IC50 / EC50** | The inflection point — the concentration at which response is halfway between Bottom and Top |
| **Hill slope** | The steepness of the sigmoidal transition |

## IC50 vs EC50

**[IC50](/reference/glossary#ic50)** and **[EC50](/reference/glossary#ec50)** are the two most common potency metrics, and they describe
opposite directions of response.

- **IC50** (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) — the concentration that inhibits 50% of the
  maximum response. Used in inhibition assays: enzyme inhibitors, ion channel blockers, cell
  viability assays. **Lower IC50 = more potent.**
- **EC50** (half-maximal effective concentration) — the concentration that produces 50% of the
  maximum activation response. Used in agonist assays: receptor activation, cell stimulation.
  **Lower EC50 = more potent.**

chemcellar records the curve type for every fitted curve (`ic50`, `ec50`, `ki`, `kd`, `ld50`,
`td50`). The curve type is set by the assay protocol and determines how the value is labelled in
reports, tables, and campaign views.

<Callout type="info">
IC50 and EC50 are not interchangeable — they describe different biological questions. Always check
the assay protocol type before comparing potency values across runs.
</Callout>

## The Hill slope

The **[Hill slope](/reference/glossary#hill-slope)** (also called the Hill coefficient) describes how steeply the
response transitions from baseline to maximum. A slope of 1 is the theoretical expectation for a
simple 1:1 ligand-receptor interaction.

| Hill slope | What it often suggests |
|------------|----------------------|
| ≈ 1 | Simple, well-behaved dose-response |
| > 1 | Cooperative binding, multiple sites, or a steep transition |
| < 1 | Heterogeneous population, partial agonism, or a shallow curve |
| < 0.5 or > 3 | Possible assay artefact; review raw data and QC metrics |

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
A Hill slope far from 1 does not automatically invalidate the result — some known drug classes
(allosteric modulators, multi-target compounds) have characteristic slopes. However, it is always
worth reviewing the raw data when you see extreme values.
</Callout>

## Asymptotes: Top and Bottom

The **Top** and **Bottom** parameters anchor the curve at its limits.

- **Top** is the response at zero compound concentration — the "vehicle control" level.
  In a well-controlled inhibition assay this should be close to 100% (or the positive control
  signal, depending on normalization).
- **Bottom** is the response at saturating compound concentration — the maximum inhibition
  achievable. In a complete inhibitor this approaches 0%. If Bottom is substantially above 0%
  (say, 30%) the compound may be a partial inhibitor or the assay may not cover a high enough
  concentration range.

Inspecting the Top and Bottom values helps you distinguish **full inhibitors** (Bottom ≈ 0%)
from **partial inhibitors** (Bottom > 0%) — a pharmacologically important distinction that a
single IC50 number hides.

## Confidence intervals and goodness of fit

Every fitted curve in chemcellar carries:

- **R²** (coefficient of determination) — how well the 4PL model fits the measured data.
  R² > 0.95 is generally acceptable; R² < 0.90 warrants inspection.
- **95% confidence interval** — the lower and upper bounds of the IC50 estimate.
  A tight CI (e.g., 42 nM, CI 38–47 nM) means the data strongly constrain the fit.
  A wide CI (e.g., 42 nM, CI 5–350 nM) indicates high uncertainty — perhaps too few
  data points, or a shallow curve.

## Curve classes

Not every compound produces a clean sigmoidal curve. chemcellar classifies each curve into one of
four classes:

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Full** | Both asymptotes are well defined; standard sigmoidal shape |
| **Partial** | Only one asymptote is reached within the concentration range tested; IC50 may be extrapolated |
| **Bell-shaped** | Response rises then falls at higher concentration — can indicate aggregation or cytotoxicity overlapping the assay signal |
| **Inactive** | No significant response across the concentration range |

Curve class is displayed prominently on every curve card. An `inactive` classification is not a
curve fit failure — it is valid scientific information meaning the compound did not inhibit (or
activate) the target at the tested concentrations.

## Qualifiers on potency values

When a compound's potency falls outside the tested concentration range, chemcellar records a
**[qualified value](/reference/glossary#qualified-value)** rather than a bare number.

| Qualifier | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| `=` | The IC50 is determined within the tested range |
| `<` | The compound is more potent than the lowest tested concentration (IC50 is below the range) |
| `>` | The compound is less potent than the highest tested concentration (inactive at the maximum dose) |
| `~` | The IC50 is an approximate extrapolation — treat with caution |

For example, a report might show IC50 > 10 000 nM to indicate that the compound showed no
meaningful inhibition at 10 µM, the assay ceiling. This is fundamentally different from IC50 =
10 000 nM (which would imply the curve plateau was measured). The qualifier preserves this
distinction throughout the database, in exports, and in campaign summaries.

## IC90 and other intercepts

Beyond IC50, medicinal chemists often need the concentration required for 90% inhibition
(**IC90**) — particularly relevant for compounds where the maximum effect matters as much as
the midpoint potency. chemcellar derives IC90 from the same 4PL fit parameters:

```
IC90 = IC50 × (9 ^ (1 / HillSlope))
```

For a standard Hill slope of 1, IC90 is exactly 9× the IC50. For steeper curves (Hill slope > 1)
the IC90/IC50 ratio is smaller — steep curves reach full inhibition more quickly.

## Excluded data points

Before a curve is fitted, individual data points can be excluded from the fit (for example, a well
that was flagged as an outlier due to a pipetting error). chemcellar stores which points were
excluded and why, alongside the fitted curve. The raw data is always preserved — exclusions affect
the fit but not the underlying measurement record.

<Callout type="info">
Excluded points are retained in the database and visible on the curve chart. They are shown as
open symbols rather than filled ones so reviewers can assess whether the exclusion was justified.
This action is recorded in the append-only audit trail.
</Callout>

## How curves are produced

chemcellar fits dose-response curves after readout data is imported into a run. The fitting
pipeline:

1. Groups readout data points by molecule, batch, and readout definition.
2. Applies outlier exclusions (if any have been flagged).
3. Runs the 4PL non-linear least-squares regression.
4. Records the four parameters (Bottom, Top, IC50/EC50, Hill slope), R², confidence intervals,
   number of points used, and the raw data array.
5. Assigns a curve class based on the fitted asymptotes and the concentration range.

Because each curve is linked to a specific run, protocol version, and batch, you can always
trace back from a potency number to the exact experiment and physical material that produced it.
